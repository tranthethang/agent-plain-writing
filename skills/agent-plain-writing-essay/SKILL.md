# Plain writing

Works in Vietnamese (`vi`), English (`en`) and Japanese (`ja`). This skill handles one document type and uses one of two voice families: `plain` or `creative`.

## Who owns what

Each rule has one owner. Read a rule in its owner file, not in a copy.

| Owner | Owns |
|---|---|
| This file, from "Workflow" to "Extend the skill" | The workflow and the core rules that hold for every type and every language |
| The voice family and type sections at the end of this file | Everything that depends on the type: voice, structure, length policy, sentence rhythm, and the kinds of phrases to ban |
| `languages/<lang>/` | Everything that depends on the language: the unit, numeric limits (`profile.json`), the actual banned phrases (`banned.txt`, `banned-guide.md`), grammar traps, pronoun and level words, terms (`terms.txt`), and examples |

**When two owners disagree:**

- On limits and structure, the type section wins over the core rules.
- On words and grammar, `languages/<lang>/` wins over the type section.
- On how much a text may add beyond the source (reasoning, reactions, inner thoughts), the voice family section and the type section win over the core rules. The facts in core rule 5 never change.
- Every limit that has a key in `profile.json` comes from `profile.json`. No other file repeats it.
- `limits` in `PLAIN-WRITING.md` override `profile.json` for that project. The checker warns when a project limit is above 1.5 times the profile value.

## Workflow

0. **Check for `PLAIN-WRITING.md`** in the project root (template: `templates/PLAIN-WRITING.example.md`). If `PLAIN-WRITING.md` exists, read it and let it win on voice and terms. Use `default_lang` and `default_type` to skip the questions in steps 1 and 2.
1. **Pick the output language** (`vi`, `en` or `ja`). It is the language of the text you produce, not the language of the prompt. A Vietnamese brief that asks for a Japanese manual means `ja`. If unclear, ask one question. Then read `languages/<lang>/profile.json` (the limits for your type) and `languages/<lang>/profile.md`.
2. **Read the type files.** This skill handles one document type. Its voice and structure are in the type section at the end of this file. Read `languages/<lang>/examples/essay.md`. Also read `languages/<lang>/banned-guide.md`. It is the banned list for the agent. `banned.txt` is the checker's machine list; do not read it. Read `languages/<lang>/terms.txt` only when the type is `user-guide` or `--non-tech` applies. Read only these.
3. **Know the reader**: who they are, what they already know, what they must do after reading. If missing, ask one question. If you cannot ask, state your assumption outside the document (or mark `TBD-n` inline in specs).
4. **Outline** (one line per idea) for any text longer than about 400 words. Write only ideas that are in the outline. In the `creative` family, the outline fixes the scenes, not every sentence.
5. **Draft** with the core rules and the type's voice, structure and rhythm. Write normal paragraphs, and never put every sentence on its own line.
6. **Edit** in a separate pass: cut, run the checklist, run the script. Fix until clean. When Python cannot run, apply the final checklist by hand and say in the report that the script did not run.
7. **Report** two things outside the generated text: the result, and what you had to guess. Never prepend or append `Result:`, `Guesses:`, or `**推測したこと:**` lines inside the document. Send the report as a second message when you can. Otherwise end the document, add one line `<!-- report -->`, then the report, and keep it out of the file that the checker reads. When you guessed nothing and added nothing, write no report and no marker. Never write a report that only says so, such as "I did not add facts beyond the brief." In specs, mark missing details inline with `TBD-n`. The script flags those labels (see "Report labels" in `banned-guide.md`) on any line outside code blocks.

Draft and edit are two passes. Do not try to get it right in the first pass.

## Core rules (both families, all languages)

These rules hold for every document type:

| # | Rule | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | One idea per sentence | In `plain`, two subordinate clauses means split. In `creative`, the type file sets sentence rhythm |
| 2 | Concrete verbs, active voice | "send", not "perform the sending of" |
| 3 | Banned phrases | Zero hits from `banned-guide.md` (the all-types section plus the section for the type) |
| 4 | Opening and closing | Opening: at most 2 sentences, straight to the point. No recap, no empty thanks |
| 5 | No invention | Add no number, event, person, place, technical spec, or outcome that is not in the source. Do not place two numbers with different units or time periods next to each other as a comparison; keep both numbers and narrow the claim to what they support. How much else a text may add (reasoning, reactions, inner thoughts) is set by its voice family |
| 6 | Consistency | One concept, one name. One register for the whole text |

## Patterns to avoid

- **Negative parallelism.** State what the thing is instead of contrasting what it is not.
  - Bad: "This is not just a refactor, it is a new foundation."
  - Good: "This refactor splits the state by form."
- **Count opening.** Start with the first point instead of announcing how many items follow.
  - Bad: "Three things to know before deploying."
  - Good: "Run the migration before deploying the worker."
- **Fake agency.** Name the person or process that acts instead of making a document or concept act.
  - Bad: "This architecture decides how requests flow."
  - Good: "The router sends each request to a worker."
- **Slogan headings and catchy labels.** Use a plain heading that names the topic.
  - Bad: "## The hidden cost of convenience"
  - Good: "## Why shared state slowed the form"
- **Invented hyphenated adjectives.** Use plain words instead of coining compound modifiers.
  - Bad: "a future-proof, developer-centric workflow"
  - Good: "a workflow that developers can extend"
- **Rhetorical question chains.** Never place two or more question sentences in a row. State the point directly.
  - Bad: "Why did it fail? Could the cache be stale?"
  - Good: "The job failed because the cache held yesterday's key."
- **Em dashes, en dashes, and middle dots.** Use a period, a comma, "and", or "to" instead of an em dash (U+2014), an en dash (U+2013), or a middle dot (U+00B7).
  - Bad: "Shared values [em dash] the user and the language [em dash] stayed global."
  - Good: "Shared values, the user and the language, stayed global."
- **Heading case and decorative bold.** Write headings in sentence case, and use bold only for UI labels or table headers.
  - Bad: "## Key Benefits Of **Fast** Caching"
  - Good: "## Benefits of caching"

## When the reader is outside the field

Common failure: the AI "translates" every technical detail into plain words. The result is three times longer and still unclear. Do the opposite:

1. Pick **at most 3 ideas** the reader needs.
2. For each idea, say **what the reader gets or can do**. Drop the internal mechanism.
3. Test each sentence: if you delete it, will the reader fail at something or misunderstand something? If not, delete it.

## Cutting order for long drafts

1. Sentences that repeat an earlier idea.
2. Sentences that explain internal mechanism.
3. The second and later examples.
4. Adjectives and adverbs.
5. Empty opening and closing.

For `tech-blog` and `essay`, keep human context, reasoning, and tension instead of cutting down to a bare fact list. In `essay`, also keep the narrator's inner thoughts and the sensory detail each scene needs.

## When editing existing text

- Keep meaning, proper names, numbers, paths and code unchanged.
- Add no new information. List ambiguous spots at the end so the author can decide.
- Give a table "original → revised → reason" if the user wants to learn from the edit.

## Final checklist

- [ ] No banned phrase from `banned-guide.md`. No empty adjective.
- [ ] None of the patterns in "Patterns to avoid" appear.

## Voice family

Numeric limits are per language and type. They live only in `languages/<lang>/profile.json`. The type section may relax or tighten a rule. See "Who owns what" for which part wins.

### `creative` (`essay`)

- **Sentence length**: `sentence` in `profile.json`.
- **Paragraph length**: `paragraph` in `profile.json`, one idea.
- **Rhythm and imagery**: Owned by the type section.
- **Evaluative adjectives**: None without a visible detail.
- **Inner voice**: the facts stay fixed (core rule 5), but the narrator may think, remember and doubt. Use the inner material the brief gives first. When the brief gives none, the type section sets how much you may add. Report each added thought outside the text (Workflow step 7).

# Essay (personal, reflective)

Use for personal essays, reflections, and short narrative pieces for general readers.

## Purpose and reader

- Reader: general readers who do not need specialized background.
- Length: open (`length` in `profile.json` is `null`). Stop when the piece lands; do not pad to hit a word count. A piece that only retells the brief summary has not landed yet: each main point needs its own scene (see Structure).

## Overrides to the core rules

- Sentences may be longer (`sentence` in `profile.json`), but **at least one third of sentences must be short** to set the rhythm.
- Paragraphs follow `paragraph` in `profile.json`.
- At least one sensory detail per paragraph (sight, sound, smell, touch, taste).
- Core rule 5 (No invention), essay reading. The facts stay fixed: do not add an event, a person, a place, a number or an outcome that is not in the brief. The texture may grow:
  - A small sensory detail that the scene already implies, for example the hum of a fan that the brief turns on, the light in a room the brief names, or the tape on a box the brief packs.
  - The narrator's inner voice. Use the thoughts, memories and doubts that the brief gives first. When the brief gives none, add at most two sentences of thought that the scene prompts. Write each as an open thought or a doubt (for example, "I still do not know if that was all he had to say."), never as a lesson or a conclusion. Report each added thought outside the text (Workflow step 7 in `SKILL.md`).
- Zero sentences that explain an emotion after the action already showed it. An open thought is not an explanation: it asks or remembers, and it does not conclude.
- Evaluative adjectives are allowed only when paired with a visible detail.

## Voice

- First person. Speak directly, without preaching.
- Keep natural narrative flow (context, tension, shift, landing). Do not restate the brief as a flat report.
- Admit mistakes or doubts when the brief has them. An honest flaw carries more weight than a neat success.
- **Show, do not tell.** Do not write "I was sad". Write what you did or saw.
- **Specific, uncommon details.** "A bowl of cold rice" beats "loneliness".
- **Do not explain the meaning.** Do not close a paragraph with "I realized that...". Let the reader realize it.
- **Vary the rhythm.** Long sentences describe; short sentences land.
- No emoji. No greeting to the audience.
- No meta-report openings (a sentence about why you write the essay) and no label closings (a closing line that starts with a label such as a lesson or a note). The phrases for each language are in `banned-guide.md`, section "Phrases of tech-blog and essay" (shared with `tech-blog`).

## Structure

- **Title**: concrete and specific. Not "Reflections on X".
- **No fixed headings**: write continuous prose with natural paragraph breaks.
- **Opening** (at most 2 sentences): start in a scene, a moment, or a concrete detail.
- **Body**: give each main point of the brief its own scene, with at least one action and one sensory detail. Do not compress a main point into one summary sentence. Before the shift, let the reader feel the tension through a brief fact (the empty days, the box not yet opened), not through a named feeling.
- **Closing** (1 or 2 sentences): close on one image or one action, not a lesson or summary. Do not restate the feeling that a main point names (for example, "the room felt like home"); show the action that makes the reader feel it.

## Brief the AI needs

1. Event, memory, or question at the center (3 to 5 lines).
2. Tone (warm, quiet, ironic, sharp).
3. One or two passages you like, marked "imitate this rhythm and word choice".
4. What must not happen or must not be said.
5. Inner material: what you thought, remembered or doubted at the time. Without it, the AI adds at most two open thoughts and reports them outside the text.

- [ ] The closing does not restate the feeling a main point names.
- [ ] The narrator has an inner voice: the thoughts the brief gives, or at most two added open thoughts, each reported outside the text.
- [ ] No inner thought states a lesson or a conclusion.
- [ ] Short sentences are mixed in to vary the rhythm.
- [ ] No paragraph ends with a sentence that explains the meaning.
- [ ] No meta-report opening, no label closing, and no banned phrase from `[all]` or `[tech-blog,essay]`.
